## Near neighbours, different rules

British senders treat France as familiar territory, and in most respects they are
right. Flowers are the exception. The etiquette here is older, firmer and more
widely known among ordinary people than ours is, and the three flowers a British
shopper reaches for without thinking are the three most likely to misfire.

## The chrysanthemum

At home it is the most ordinary bunch in the shop: cheap, long-lasting, sold by the
armful at petrol stations. In France it belongs to the first of November, when
families carry potted chrysanthemums to the graves of their relatives and the growers
plan the whole year around that single week. It has essentially no other public life.
Sent to a flat for a birthday it reads as a message about death.

## The carnation

Another British staple with a French shadow. The oeillet is believed to bring bad
luck; it is the flower you do not take to a dinner, and certain professions will not
have it in the building. Whether or not the person you are sending to holds the
superstition, there is no cost at all to choosing something else, and a fleuriste
will quietly do so on your behalf.

## Yellow, and counting

Yellow keeps an old link to infidelity, so an all-yellow romantic bouquet is a poor
idea and an all-yellow housewarming bunch is perfectly fine. Counting is milder: give
an odd number, avoid thirteen, and be aware that the British dozen is faintly
off-key. A shop composes to a shape rather than a count, so the problem disappears
if you leave the arrangement to it.

## The first of May

The loveliest custom in the country and the one no British sender anticipates. On
that morning everybody gives everybody a sprig of muguet, lily of the valley, for
luck. It is sold in the street by anyone who wants to sell it, tolerated outside the
normal rules of trade, and by lunchtime half the country is carrying a sprig. It is
also a public holiday, so the shops themselves are closed; the days either side are
the practical window.

## The rest of the calendar

The Fete des Meres is the last Sunday in May unless Pentecost falls there, in which
case it moves to the first Sunday in June: 31 May in 2026, 30 May in 2027. The Fete
des Peres is the third Sunday in June, and the Fete des Grands-Meres is the first
Sunday in March.

Public holidays shut the shops: the 1st of January, Easter Monday, the 1st and 8th
of May, Ascension, Whit Monday, the 14th of July, the 15th of August, the 1st and
11th of November, and the 25th of December. Alsace and the Moselle keep Good Friday
and the 26th of December too. August closes smaller towns almost entirely.

## What a fleuriste is good at

Restraint and proportion. A composition here is smaller, more deliberate and better
finished than the equivalent spend buys at home: a clear silhouette, a limited
palette, good material, paper folded rather than crumpled. Ask for something de
saison and you will be looked after. Ask for a hundred roses and you will get them,
and the gesture will be louder than the sentiment.
