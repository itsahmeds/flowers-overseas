## Two absolutes, and a pleasure

Most of the flower etiquette we write about is a matter of degree. Italy has two
rules that are not. Learn them and the rest of the country is easy.

**The chrysanthemum is the flower of the dead.** On the first and second of
November families fill the cemeteries with them, and at every funeral in between
they are there again. There is no autumnal reading available, no charming exception,
no regional variation to fall back on. A British sender thinks of it as the cheapest
cheerful bunch in the shop, which is exactly why this is the error we expect.

**Stems are counted.** Odd numbers for the living, even numbers for the dead. The
British dozen, the default romantic gesture at home, is the wrong count. A younger
recipient in Milan may shrug; an aunt in Bari certainly will not. A shop applies the
rule automatically, so the only way to get it wrong is to insist.

The pleasure is the eighth of March. On the Festa della Donna, sprigs of mimosa are
handed to women everywhere by colleagues, sons, friends and strangers. It is not
romantic and it is not really optional. Growers on the Ligurian coast time their
entire season to that morning. If you are sending to a woman in Italy in early
March, mimosa is the gift, and roses that week will look like you were not paying
attention.

## Name days, which Britain does not have

Every day belongs to one or more saints, and the person carrying that name is
congratulated by family, neighbours and colleagues. In a southern family the
onomastico can outrank the birthday. Flowers are the ordinary gift for it, on a
modest scale: a bunch rather than an installation. Since we have nothing like it at
home, it is the easiest way for a British sender to look thoughtful rather than
merely dutiful.

## The calendar, and the closures

The Festa della Mamma is the second Sunday in May and is the heaviest ordinary
flower date of the year. The 19th of March, San Giuseppe, is fathers' day and belongs
more to fried pastry than to bouquets. The Festa dei Nonni, for grandparents, is the
2nd of October.

The national holidays are the 1st and 6th of January, Easter Monday, the 25th of
April, the 1st of May, the 2nd of June, the 15th of August, the 1st of November, the
8th of December and the 25th and 26th of December. On top of those, every city keeps
its patron saint, and those days close everything locally.

Ferragosto is the one that strands British senders. Around the 15th of August a great
deal of the country leaves for the sea, and a small shop in a city may shut for a
fortnight with nothing but a note in the window. If your date is in mid-August,
assume the smaller shops are away.

## What an Italian shop is good at

Shape, above all. The style is more sculpted than the loose paper bunch a British
florist produces: a clear silhouette, considered proportions, fewer varieties handled
better. Seasonal material is genuinely seasonal, and the ranunculus and anemones
that come off the Ligurian coast in late winter are worth asking for by name. A
potted cyclamen in the cold months is an ordinary, welcome gift that senders from
here rarely think of.
