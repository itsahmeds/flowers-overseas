## The easiest destination we write about, with one catch

There is very little to get wrong here. Almost nothing is taboo, nobody counts
stems, the shops are good and the material is fresher and cheaper than a British
sender is used to. The catch is scale, and it is worth a paragraph of explanation
because it is not obvious.

Flowers here are not an occasion purchase. They come home from the market on a
Saturday with the bread and the cheese, they go into a vase in the front window
where the street can see them, and they are replaced the following week. A home
without something in that window in February is unusual. Against that background, a
towering arrangement in three layers of cellophane does not read as generosity; it
reads as somebody who has misjudged the room. A good seasonal bunch, loosely tied,
with the stems showing, is the local idea of a lovely gift, and it costs less than
the thing you were about to order.

## The tulip year, honestly

Forced tulips appear in January, and the third Saturday of that month is National
Tulip Day, when a picking garden is built on the Dam in Amsterdam and people queue to
pick a free bunch. That is the opening of the commercial season, not of the fields.

The fields are a short and specific pleasure. Depending on the winter, the bulb
region behind the dunes colours up from about the middle of April and is usually
topped by early May, because the bulb is the crop. Those famous stripes of colour are
mown down in their prime so the bulb can fatten. Photographs of them dated late May are
either a different crop or a different year, and British visitors booking a trip on
the strength of them are often a fortnight late.

Hyacinths and narcissi run slightly ahead of the tulips and smell far better. Peonies
take over in June. Dahlias do the work of the late summer, and through the winter the
amaryllis and the hyacinth bulb in a glass are on every second windowsill.

## The dates that matter

Moederdag, the second Sunday in May, is the heaviest gift date of the year, and it is
about two months after Mothering Sunday at home. Vaderdag is the third Sunday in
June, which happens to coincide with the British date, and is a much quieter affair.

Koningsdag on the 27th of April, moved to the 26th when the date falls on a Sunday,
turns the whole country orange and closes a great many shops. Orange flowers sell out
in the days before it.

The 4th and 5th of May sit oddly together. Dodenherdenking on the 4th is the
remembrance of the war dead, with wreaths at memorials and two minutes of silence at
eight in the evening. The 5th, Liberation Day, is festive. The first is not a day for
sending a cheerful bouquet to a house.

Sinterklaas on the 5th of December is a gift occasion but not a flower one: it runs on
poems, sweets and small presents, and the shops are busy with everything but
bouquets.

## How the shops and markets work

Most towns have a market on a fixed weekday, and the stalls are as good as the shops
and often better. City centres and supermarkets frequently open on Sundays, which is
not true across most of the region, so a Sunday gift is genuinely possible in
Amsterdam or Rotterdam and much less so in a village. Public holidays are the real
closure, and Koningsdag is the biggest of them.
