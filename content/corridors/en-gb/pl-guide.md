## Written for a sender in Britain

There is a version of this guide for readers anywhere, and this is the version for
people sending from here. The difference is not spelling. It is that almost every
assumption a British sender carries into a Polish flower shop is slightly wrong, and
the errors are all recoverable if you know about them in advance.

## The dates do not line up

Mothering Sunday moves with Lent and lands in March. Dzien Matki is fixed on the
26th of May and does not move at all. Nothing connects the two, and every year we
expect to meet people who marked the wrong one. Put the May date in the calendar
and forget the British one for this purpose.

The eighth of March, Dzien Kobiet, has no British counterpart whatsoever. Men give
flowers to colleagues, neighbours and relatives, and the scale is small: one tulip,
a few carnations. Sending an enormous romantic arrangement to a colleague that day
misreads the occasion by some distance.

The first of November is the heaviest day of the year. Families travel across the
country to clean and dress graves, and they carry chrysanthemums to do it. The
cemeteries are lit and full, the shops sell out, and nothing else moves.

The twenty-fourth of December, Wigilia, is the Christmas Eve supper, and the house
is cooking from dawn. Flowers earlier in that week are a kindness; flowers on the
day are one more thing to find a vase for.

## The counting, and the British dozen

A dozen roses is the default romantic gesture at home. It is the wrong count there.
An odd number is for a living person and an even number is for a grave, and this is
not a folk memory that has faded: shop staff apply it without being asked, and a bunch
that arrives with an even count will be noticed. If we compose the bouquet, the shop
does the counting and you do not need to think about it.

## Name days, which we simply do not have

Every day in the calendar carries a set of saints' names, and the person whose name
it is gets congratulated by everybody around them. Plenty of people treat their
imieniny more warmly than their birthday. There is no equivalent here, so it is the
easiest thoughtful gesture available to a British sender: find the date from the
first name, and send something modest on it.

## What a shop there will build

Loose, soft, hand-tied bunches, wrapped in paper and tied with ribbon. Seasonal
material is genuinely seasonal, so tulips carry the spring, peonies own the turn of
May and June, and asters and dahlias take the autumn. Ask for something in season
and you will get better flowers for the same outlay than you will by insisting on
roses in January. Colour is used more boldly than a British eye expects, and that is
usually the right instinct rather than the wrong one.

## Money, and where you stand

You pay in pounds sterling. You are shown one figure, with VAT and delivery already
inside it, before you enter a card, and that figure is what leaves your account. No
currency conversion is applied afterwards and no fee appears at the end. The person
receiving the flowers is never shown a price, and there is no paperwork in the box.

Your contract is with us, not with the shop that makes the bouquet. If something is
wrong you complain to us and we sort it out; you never have to take up an argument with a shop
abroad in a language you do not speak.

## What we can honestly tell you today

We are not delivering to Poland yet. We have written this guide, chosen the cities we
want to begin in, and we are talking to the shops we would like to make your flowers.
Until one of them has agreed to work with us there will be no date and no hour on this
page. When that changes, this section is the first thing that changes with it.
