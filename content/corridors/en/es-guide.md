## One country, several flower cultures

It is tempting to write about Spain as a single market and wrong to do it. The
customs that govern a gift in Catalonia are not the ones a family in Galicia keeps,
and the calendar a shop in Seville works to is shaped by processions that do not
exist in Bilbao. What follows is the common ground, with the regional exceptions
called out where they matter.

The common ground starts with the carnation. Elsewhere it is a flower with an
awkward reputation; here it is simply a good flower, grown in quantity, used in
everything, and given without a second thought. If you want an arrangement that
looks and feels local rather than imported, carnations are the honest answer.

The second piece of common ground is the saint. Your santo is the feast day of
whoever you were named after, and in a great many families it is marked with the
same warmth as a birthday. Nobody will be offended if you do not know it, and
everybody is pleased when you do.

The third is the cemetery flower. Chrysanthemums belong to Todos los Santos, the
first of November, when families clean and dress graves. Sending them to a home is
the one clear error available to a well-meaning foreigner.

## The twenty-third of April

Sant Jordi is the day worth understanding. In Catalonia, on the feast of Saint
George, people give each other a book and a rose. Stalls appear on every corner in
Barcelona, the Rambla becomes impassable, and the rose trade does more business in
those hours than on any other date in the year. Outside Catalonia the day passes
unremarked, so it is a wonderful gesture for somebody in Girona or Tarragona and a
slightly baffling one for somebody in Seville.

## The rest of the year

Dia de la Madre is the first Sunday in May. Dia del Padre is 19 March, San Jose,
a public holiday in some regions and an ordinary one in others. Dia de los Abuelos,
for grandparents, is 26 July. Todos los Santos is 1 November and is about graves.

Semana Santa reshapes spring completely. The brotherhoods dress their floats in
carnations, lilies and, in Andalusia, azahar, the orange blossom whose scent is the
smell of the city that week, and every shop in town is working to that deadline
rather than to yours. Expect shortened hours, expect processions to close the
streets, and expect a florist to be candid with you about what is possible.

The national holidays to plan around are 1 and 6 January, Good Friday, 1 May,
15 August, 12 October, 1 November, 6 and 8 December, and 25 December. On top of
those, every region and almost every town keeps its own fiesta, and on that day the
shutters come down whatever the national calendar says.

## Heat, hours and what a shop will agree to

From June to September the heat decides. A good Spanish florist will not sell you
something soft-headed for a flat with no air conditioning, and if you insist they
will tell you what will happen to it. Take the advice. Potted plants, succulents,
carnations and firm-headed mixed bunches are what survive, and they are what an
experienced shop will push you towards.

Hours are the other thing to know. Outside the largest cities a shop may close in
the early afternoon and reopen in the evening, and Sunday trading is restricted.
Madrid, Barcelona and Valencia are the exceptions rather than the rule.
