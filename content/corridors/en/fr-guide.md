## A country with rules about flowers

Nowhere else we write about has quite this much etiquette attached to a bunch of
stems. It is not snobbery; it is simply that the meanings have been stable for a
long time and everybody knows them. Learn four of them and you will never send
anything embarrassing.

The first is the chrysanthemum, and it is absolute. On the first of November,
la Toussaint, the whole country visits its cemeteries carrying potted
chrysanthemums, and growers plan their entire year around that one week. The
flower has no other public life. A beautiful arrangement of them sent to an
apartment for a birthday reads as a message about death, not about autumn.

The second is the carnation, the oeillet. There is a long-standing belief that
it brings bad luck, strong enough that it is the one flower you never carry to a
dinner. Some trades will not have them in the building at all. Whether or not the
person you are sending to holds the superstition, there is no cost to avoiding it.

The third is colour. Yellow has an old association with infidelity, which is why a
shop will steer a romantic order away from an all-yellow composition. In a mixed
bunch it means nothing, and for a housewarming or a new baby it is entirely fine.
But if the flowers are meant to say something tender, keep them out of that corner
of the spectrum.

The fourth is counting. Give an odd number, and avoid thirteen. That is the whole
rule, and in practice a fleuriste composes to a shape rather than to a count, so
if you leave the composition to the shop it will be handled for you.

## Muguet, and the one morning nobody can regulate

The first of May is the loveliest flower custom in Europe. People give each other a
sprig of lily of the valley, muguet, for luck. The sale of it on that day is
tolerated outside the normal rules of trade, so it is sold from folding tables, from
doorways and by children on corners, and by lunchtime half the country is carrying
a sprig. It is also a public holiday, which means the shops themselves are shut. If
you want to mark it for somebody, the days on either side are the practical window.

## The rest of the calendar

The Fete des Meres is the last Sunday in May unless that Sunday is Pentecost, in
which case it moves to the first Sunday in June; 31 May in 2026, 30 May in 2027.
The Fete des Peres is the third Sunday in June. The Fete des Grands-Meres, the
first Sunday in March, began as an advertising idea and has quietly become real.

Public holidays close the shops: 1 January, Easter Monday on 6 April in 2026 and
29 March in 2027, 1 May, 8 May, Ascension on 14 May in 2026 and 6 May in 2027,
Whit Monday, 14 July, 15 August, 1 and 11 November, and 25 December. Alsace and the
Moselle add Good Friday and 26 December, a survival of their own history.

August is its own kind of closure. In smaller towns a fleuriste may shut for two or
three weeks and put a handwritten sign in the window, and there is nothing to be
done about it except to know it happens.

## What a French shop is good at

Restraint, mostly. A composition here tends to be smaller and more deliberate than
the armful you might expect: a clear shape, a limited palette, good material, and
paper folded rather than crumpled around it. Ask for something de saison and you
will be looked after. Ask for a hundred roses and you will get them, but the person
receiving them may find the gesture louder than the sentiment behind it.

Regional habits vary more than you would think. Provence leans on lavender,
sunflowers and field material through the summer; Paris and Lyon keep the tighter,
more formal style all year; the Atlantic coast does hydrangeas better than anywhere.
