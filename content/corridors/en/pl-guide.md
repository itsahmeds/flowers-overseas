## Why we started here

Poland is where this began. It is the country we get asked about most, it is where
we have spent the most time, and it is the market whose flower culture is the least
like the one most of our customers grew up in. That last point is the reason this
page exists. Flowers carry meaning, and the meaning changes at the border. Get it
wrong and you have sent a message you did not intend to send, in a language you did
not know you were speaking.

## What flowers mean there

The first thing to know is the chrysanthemum. In many countries it is an ordinary
autumn flower. There, it is the flower of the cemetery, carried in armfuls to family
graves on 1 November. A gorgeous bouquet of them delivered to an office or a kitchen
table will not read as generous; it will read as a mistake, or worse, as a message
about mortality. The same caution applies to lilies, though less strongly.

The second is counting. Give an odd number of stems to a living person and an even
number only at a funeral or a graveside. This is not a quaint survival that nobody
observes any more: it is a live convention that shop staff apply without being asked,
and a bunch of a dozen roses is the sort of thing that gets quietly re-counted before
it goes out of the door. When we make a bouquet for you, the shop counts it correctly
as a matter of course.

The third is that flowers there are given constantly, and not only romantically. A
teacher is thanked with flowers. A doctor is thanked with flowers. A host is brought
flowers at the door, unwrapped from the paper before they are handed over. Small,
frequent and sincere beats large and occasional.

## The days that matter

Four dates shape the year. On 8 March, Dzień Kobiet, men give flowers to the women
around them; a single tulip is a complete gift that day, and the shops know it. On
26 May, Dzień Matki, everyone gives flowers to their mother, and the date does not
move — a fixed day, unlike the wandering Sundays used in Britain and the United
States. On 1 November, Wszystkich Świętych, the country travels to its cemeteries;
it is the heaviest flower day of the year and the one when a shop will politely tell
you it has nothing left. And on 24 December, Wigilia, the Christmas Eve supper, homes
are dressed with poinsettias and the last of the season's greenery.

Around those sit the public holidays, which close shops and slow everything down:
1 and 6 January, Easter Sunday and Easter Monday — 5 and 6 April in 2026, 28 and 29
March in 2027 — 1 and 3 May, Pentecost and Corpus Christi, 15 August, 1 and 11
November, and 25 and 26 December. If the day you have in mind is one of those, plan
around it rather than through it.

Then there are name days. The Polish calendar gives every day of the year a set of
saints' names, and the person whose name it is will be wished well by family,
colleagues and neighbours. Plenty of people mark their imieniny more warmly than
their birthday, and flowers are the standard gift for it. If you know only a first
name, you can find the day; if you know the day, you know what to send.

## What a Polish florist is good at

The shops we like there — a kwiaciarnia is a florist's shop — build loose, soft,
hand-tied bunches, wrapped in paper and tied with ribbon, rather than the packed
foam arrangements common elsewhere. Seasonal work is genuinely seasonal: tulips
carry the spring, peonies own late May and June, and dahlias and asters take over
in the autumn. Ask for something in season and you will get better flowers for the
same money than you would by insisting on roses in January.
