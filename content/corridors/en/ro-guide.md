## A country where flowers are ordinary and constant

The habit here is closer to daily life than to special occasions. Somebody visiting
a home brings flowers. A pupil brings flowers to a teacher, and on the last day of
term the staff room disappears behind them. A woman marking her name day will be
handed several bunches during the working hours of a single Tuesday. If you are
sending from abroad, the useful consequence is that you do not need an excuse.

The taste is generous. A bouquet is expected to be substantial, densely built and
elaborately wrapped, and what would look extravagant in a northern city looks
normal here. A modest posy, sent with great affection, can read as an afterthought
to somebody used to the local scale.

## The first of March

Martisor is the custom nobody outside the region quite expects. On the first of
March a small charm is worn on a twisted red and white thread, given by men to
women, by children to teachers, by friends to friends, and it is pinned on and worn
for the first days of the month. The flowers that go with it are the first of the
year: snowdrops above all, then hyacinths and the earliest freesias.

A week later comes the eighth, Ziua Femeii, which the country keeps with real
enthusiasm and which many families still treat as the day for mothers even though
the official Ziua Mamei now falls on the first Sunday in May. Between the two dates
the shops work harder than at any other point before Easter.

## An Easter on its own calendar

The Orthodox reckoning puts Easter on 12 April in 2026 and on 2 May in 2027, and
the whole spring is arranged around it. The Sunday before, Florii, is Palm Sunday,
when willow branches are blessed; it is also the collective name day of everybody
whose name comes from a flower, and there are a great many of them. Fifty days
after Easter comes Rusalii, Pentecost, another public holiday.

Name days generally are the thing a foreigner most often misses. Sfantul Ion on
7 January accounts for an enormous number of people; Sfantul Gheorghe on 23 April
and Sfintii Constantin si Elena on 21 May do the same; and Sfanta Maria on
15 August is so widely shared that shops order for it as they would for a national
holiday. If you know somebody's first name you can find their day, and marking it
will mean more than a card on a birthday.

## The days the country stops

The public holidays are 1 and 2 January, Boboteaza on 6 January and Sfantul Ion on
the 7th, 24 January, Orthodox Good Friday, Easter Sunday and Monday, 1 May, 1 June
for children, Rusalii and the Monday after it, 15 August, 30 November for Sfantul
Andrei, 1 December for the national day, and 25 and 26 December. That is a long
list, and the first days of January and the whole week of Easter are the two
stretches when almost nothing is open.

Luminatia, on 1 November, is kept most strongly in Transylvania, where cemeteries
are lit with candles and dressed with chrysanthemums. It is the reason the flower
reads the way it does, and the reason to keep it away from a living room.

## What a Romanian shop is good at

Volume and wrapping, done properly. A florist in Bucharest or Cluj will build
something large, symmetrical and heavily finished, with ribbon, paper and often a
sleeve. Roses are the staple, available all year and used for everything from a
graduation to an apology. Spring is the strongest season, when tulips, hyacinths
and freesias arrive in quantity and prices for them fall to their lowest.
