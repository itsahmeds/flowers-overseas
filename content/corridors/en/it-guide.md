## Two rules, learned in a minute

If you remember nothing else about giving flowers here, remember the chrysanthemum
and the counting.

The chrysanthemum is the flower of the dead. On the first and second of November
families fill the cemeteries with them, and at every funeral in between they are
there again. There is no charming autumnal reading available. An arrangement of
them sent to a kitchen table is not a mistake that can be laughed off, and no
fiorista would let it leave the shop for a birthday.

The counting is almost as firm. Odd numbers are for the living and even numbers are
for the dead. It is the sort of convention that a younger recipient in Milan may
shrug at and an aunt in Bari certainly will not, and since a shop applies it
automatically there is no reason to test it.

## The eighth of March

The Festa della Donna is the day the trade plans its spring around. Mimosa, the
fluffy yellow acacia that flowers in late winter, is given to women everywhere:
colleagues to colleagues, sons to mothers, friends to friends. It is not romantic
and it is not optional in the way other gestures are. Growers on the Ligurian
coast time their whole season to that morning, and when the weather has been poor
the whole country notices.

If you are sending to a woman in Italy in early March, mimosa is the gift. Sending
roses that week instead will read as a misunderstanding of what the day is for.

## Name days, and why a birthday is not the whole story

Every day in the calendar belongs to one or more saints, and the person carrying
that name is congratulated by family, neighbours and colleagues. In a southern
family an onomastico can be the bigger of the two celebrations. Flowers are the
ordinary gift for it, on a modest scale: a bunch, not an installation.

## The year, and the days everything stops

The Festa della Mamma is the second Sunday in May and is the heaviest ordinary
flower date of the year. The 19th of March, San Giuseppe, is fathers' day, and
belongs more to fried pastry than to bouquets. The Festa dei Nonni, for
grandparents, is 2 October.

The national holidays are 1 January, 6 January, Easter Monday, 25 April, 1 May,
2 June, 15 August, 1 November, 8 December, and 25 and 26 December. On top of them
every city keeps its patron: Sant'Ambrogio closes Milan on 7 December, San Giovanni
closes Florence and Turin on 24 June, Saints Peter and Paul close Rome on 29 June,
San Gennaro closes Naples on 19 September. A local holiday is as closed as a
national one.

Then there is Ferragosto. Around the 15th of August much of the country leaves for
the sea, and a small shop in a city may simply shut for a fortnight. If the date you
care about is in mid-August, assume the smaller shops are away.

## What an Italian shop is good at

Shape. The style here is more sculpted than the loose paper-wrapped bunch you find
further north: a clear silhouette, considered proportions, and a preference for
fewer varieties handled well. Seasonal material is genuinely seasonal, and the
Ligurian coast supplies ranunculus and anemones that are worth asking for in late
winter. Potted cyclamen in the cold months is an ordinary, welcome gift that
foreigners rarely think of.
