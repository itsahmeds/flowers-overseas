## Flowers as groceries

The most useful thing to understand about this country is that flowers are not a
special-occasion purchase. They are bought at the market on a Saturday with the
bread and the cheese, they cost very little, and they go into a vase in the front
window where the street can see them. A home without something in that window in
February is unusual.

This changes what a gift means. Sending a vast, heavily wrapped construction will
not be read as generosity so much as a misreading of the setting. A good seasonal
bunch, loosely tied, with the stems visible, is the local idea of a lovely gift.
Restraint here is not meanness; it is fluency.

It also means the standard is high and the prices are low. Material moves fast, the
growers are close, and the trade around Aalsmeer sets the tone for the whole
continent. A bloemist expects you to know roughly what is in season and will happily
tell you when you do not.

## The tulip year, honestly

Forced tulips appear in January, and the third Saturday of that month is National
Tulip Day, when a temporary picking garden is built on the Dam in Amsterdam and
people queue to pick a free bunch. That is the opening of the commercial season, not
of the fields.

The fields themselves are a short and specific pleasure. Depending on the winter,
the bulb region behind the dunes colours up from about the middle of April and is
usually topped by early May, because the flower is not the crop. The bulb is. This
is the thing visitors find hardest to accept: those famous stripes of colour are cut
off in their prime so the bulb can fatten, and photographs of them dated late May are
either a different crop or a different year.

Hyacinths and narcissi run slightly ahead of the tulips and smell far better. Peonies
take over in June. In the late summer the dahlia does most of the work, and through
the winter the amaryllis and the hyacinth bulb in a glass are on every second
windowsill.

## The dates that matter

Moederdag is the second Sunday in May and is the heaviest gift date of the year.
Vaderdag is the third Sunday in June and is a much quieter affair. Koningsdag, on
27 April, moved to the 26th when the date falls on a Sunday, turns the whole country
orange and shuts a great many shops; orange flowers sell out beforehand.

The fourth and fifth of May sit oddly together. Dodenherdenking on the fourth is the
remembrance of the war dead, marked by wreaths at memorials and two minutes of
silence at eight in the evening; the fifth is Liberation Day, which is festive. The
first is not a day for sending a cheerful bouquet to a house.

Sinterklaas, on the fifth of December, is a gift occasion but not a flower one: it
runs on poems, sweets and small presents. The shops are busy with everything except
bouquets.

## How the shops and markets work

Most towns have a market on a fixed weekday and stalls that sell as well as any
shop. City centres and supermarkets often open on Sundays, which is not the case in
most of the region, so a Sunday gift is genuinely possible in Amsterdam or Rotterdam
and much less so in a village. Public holidays are the real closure, and Koningsdag
is the biggest of them.
